# GreenSolver — architecture and design rationale

`guliwerr/solvers/green/@GreenSolver/` (`GreenSolver.calc`, called via `Calc.*`/`GreenSolver.calc` directly). Computes the surface dynamic Green's function G_IJ(k∥, ω) for a half-space or layered anisotropic solid — an independent physical cross-check against Ritz, and the basis of response prediction for transient grating spectroscopy (TGS) and surface Brillouin scattering (SurfBS).

By definition, this is the response to a spatially and temporally harmonic (**plane-wave**) source at a given in-plane wavevector and frequency — the same plane-wave assumption Christoffel and Ritz make from their own directions (`docs/background.md`, "The physics").

## What it computes, and why abs(G) is the default

G_IJ(k∥, ω) is the surface displacement in direction J from a unit spatially/temporally periodic surface force in direction I, as a function of in-plane wavevector and frequency. In the normalised convention used throughout (`|k∥|=1`, ω expressed as a velocity), the velocity axis and the G spectrum are directly comparable.

The spectrum has three feature types: a sharp SAW peak (the boundary-condition matrix is nearly singular there — the pole is regularised by the limiting-absorption complex frequency described below; it sits exactly at a free surface wave, since `det B = 0` with no load is the free-mode condition of the partial-wave method, Farnell 1970, Eq. 8), a broader pseudo-SAW peak (radiates into the bulk, Lim & Farnell 1969; so the determinant is small but non-zero), and step-like kinks at bulk-wave threshold slownesses.

`abs(G_IJ)` is the primary observable and default plotting/mode-ID quantity throughout GuliweRR — TGS measures the magnitude of the ±k-combined surface response (Maznev, Akthakul & Nelson 1999, Eqs. 6–8; Grabec et al., *Nat. Commun.* (2026, accepted), arXiv:2510.10696). `Im(G_33)` is kept as a secondary output (the surface Brillouin scattering (SurfBS) spectrum is ∝ ω⁻¹ Im G_33; Every 2002, p. R27). For TGS the physically relevant component is G_13 (horizontal force → normal displacement, the default `forceDir=1, displDir=3`); G_33 (normal → normal) is symmetric under k→−k for any elastic symmetry, needs no ±k handling. The Calc classes' stored Green data and the viewers' default Green display are |G_13,eff| (below), not G_33. Surface-wave *existence* in `ModeClassification` is decided from the ±k-symmetrised G_13 (G13_eff: `GreenScore`, `GreenScoreLayered`, `GreenSpotScore`), not from G_33: why G_13 and not G_33 is explained in `docs/reference/architecture/forwardCalcs_modeClassification.md`, "`SawSpectrum` — the shared late-time machinery".

## Algorithm outline

**Unified limiting-absorption damping.** Every quantity below (`k3`, `U`, `B`, `G`) is computed at a single complex frequency `ω → ω·√(1+i·η)`, `η=LIMITING_ABSORPTION_ETA=3e-4` — limiting absorption, the standard retarded-Green's-function prescription `ω → ω+i0⁺`, applied throughout the whole solve. Every et al. 1997 use `+i0⁺` only to make the frequency integral converge (Eq. 21); they pick the outgoing partial waves at real ω by ray direction (bulk roots) and decay (evanescent roots) (p. 1347), and broaden the Rayleigh pole separately (Eq. 33, p. 1349). The two choices agree as η → 0⁺: damping moves a real bulk root by `δk3 ≈ i·ωη/(2·v_g3)`, with `v_g3` the depth component of its group velocity, so `Im(k3)>0` keeps exactly the wave whose ray points into the solid. They differ only where `v_g3 = 0` (degenerate roots, limiting velocities); there the ray rule is undefined and the damped one is not. One mechanism therefore both selects the substrate partial waves (step 3) and regularises the Rayleigh pole. The full-output fields (`.dispField`/`.depthAxis`, layered `.k3OutLayered`/`.polarizationULayered`/`.partialAmplitudeLayered`, via `nargout>1`) report the same damped solve as `.G`.

**η is set by peak-lineshape convergence, not classification margin.** Any η from 1e-5 to 1e-3 separates the partial waves cleanly. What fixes the value is that a bound Rayleigh peak is a pole of the undamped problem: at very small η its sampled height depends on where a velocity point lands relative to the pole and does not converge with refinement. A larger η broadens the pole into a finite, sampling-independent peak (height × FWHM conserved, location unaffected) but also inflates the linewidth of genuinely leaky modes. 3e-4 is the compromise (computed with GuliweRR): residual sampling noise on a bound peak of a few per cent. The damping adds to a leaky pole's own half-width γ rather than scaling it: a pole at v₀ − iγ appears with FWHM(|G|) = 2√3·(γ + ηv/2), so 3e-4 adds about 1 m/s to the FWHM at 2 km/s. What that does relative to the true linewidth depends on the mode. A broad pseudo-SAW barely changes. A narrow one can be dominated by η: on Cu(001) at 30° (γ ≈ 0.12–0.2 m/s) the FWHM grows 2.7–3.9×. Absolute peak-height thresholds applied to the default-η spectrum (e.g. `GreenPeaks.find`'s `HEIGHT_FRAC`) depend on this lineshape and need re-checking if η changes. The Name-Value option `'Eta'` of `GreenSolver.calc` overrides the default per call; a larger value emulates a finite measurement linewidth (a simple pole's |G| has FWHM ≈ √3·η·v — derived here: ω → ω√(1+iη) ≈ ω(1+iη/2) moves the pole to v₀ − iηv₀/2, so |G| ∝ 1/√(Δ² + γ²) with γ = ηv/2, which falls to half its maximum at |Δ| = √3·γ, giving FWHM = 2√3·γ = √3·η·v; for |G|² the same steps give η·v — so a measured relative linewidth Δv/v corresponds to η ≈ Δv/(√3·v)).

**The classification uses a larger η.** The Calc classes' `'full'` solve runs G_13 at `eta = SawSpectrum.ETA = 3e-3` (`GreenSolver.calc(structRot, 0, velAxis, 1, 3, 'Eta', 3e-3)`, scoring `G_eff`; `CalcCommon.greenScoreSpectrum`), and `GreenSpotScore` solves its spot directions at its own `ETA = 7e-3`; why, and how the thresholds are calibrated, is in `forwardCalcs_modeClassification.md` (section 5, "η = 3e-3, not the solver default 3e-4"). Everything the Calc classes store, draw or recompute on request (`|G13_eff|`, `greenComponent`/`greenComponentMap`) is therefore at η = 3e-3; code that needs the default-η lineshape calls `GreenSolver.calc` or `CalcCommon.greenSpectrum` itself.

For each velocity `v` in the scan range, with normalised slowness `k1=+1, k2=0, ω=v·√(1+i·η)` (the −k side follows by reciprocity, below):

1. **Find k3**: `det|C_ijkl s_j s_k − ρ δ_il| = 0` is, for fixed in-plane slowness, a 6th-degree polynomial in k3 — Every et al. 1997 (Eq. 10) call this "the characteristic sextic equation". It follows directly from the Christoffel equation once the in-plane slowness is fixed; keeping three of its roots and imposing the free-surface traction is the partial-wave method of surface-wave theory in anisotropic crystals (Farnell 1970, Eqs. 4′–8). This codebase solves it directly as a polynomial: matrix-polynomial determinant, then the roots of every point of the velocity axis at once — one batched `pageeig` over the companion matrices, built exactly as `roots()` builds its own (bit-identical roots, same order; a point whose leading or trailing coefficient is zero still goes through `roots()` itself).
2. **Find polarisations U** (`Calc_U_allVel`, one batched call per layer for the whole velocity axis): each k3 gives a polarisation vector as the null vector of `Γ(k) − ρω²I`. Non-degenerate case: the largest row cross product of the rank-2 matrix `Γ − ρω²I` (bilinear, no conjugation), normalised — the same ray as an SVD's null vector, without the SVD; its overall phase is arbitrary and cancels in every `G` and in `U·A` (the stored `polarizationULayered` is only meaningful together with `partialAmplitudeLayered`). A numerically rank-1 page (a double root that was not paired, only at eta ≲ 1e-9) falls back to the SVD. Degenerate case (repeated k3): see "Near-isotropic materials" below.
3. **Select 3 physical partial waves** of the 6 k3 roots: `Im(k3)>0`, unconditionally — no threshold. With Im ω > 0 no root can be real (a real k3 would be a lossless bulk wave at complex ω), so no root crosses the real axis as v changes and the 3/3 split of the undamped decaying/outgoing choice (Every et al. 1997, pp. 1346–1347) persists at every v. An evanescence or group-velocity test would be ill-posed whenever two roots are exactly or nearly degenerate (always the case for isotropic materials between the bulk speeds, and possible at isolated directions in any anisotropic material), which is why the damped sextic is used instead — see "Near-isotropic materials" below. If a point ever does not split 3/3, the three roots with the largest `Im(k3)` are taken and one consolidated `GreenSolver:calc:absorptionFallback` warning is issued.
4. **Build the boundary-condition matrix B** (traction contribution of each partial wave at the free surface, Every 2015 Eq. 5) and invert it. For a half-space B is 3×3 and is inverted directly (`inv(B)`, all nine elements enter the G sum). For a layered stack only the first three columns of the inverse are ever read (the force direction runs over 1–3), so `calc.m` solves `B \ eye(n,3)` instead of forming the full inverse (see "Conditioning" below).
5. **Compute G_IJ** = `(i/ω) Σ_n (B⁻¹)_{n,I} · U(J,n)` — `ω`'s own imaginary part regularises the sum at the pole; no separate damping step.

**Layered extension** (Every 2002): the layer/substrate interface sits at a local x3=0 with the free surface at x3=−h — the opposite depth orientation from the rest of the codebase; this is fully internal to `calc.m`, not something callers need to track. For N film layers, the system is `(6N+3)×(6N+3)`: 6 wave amplitudes per film plus 3 substrate amplitudes, assembled from traction-free (free surface), displacement-continuity, and traction-continuity blocks at each interface. Built as one direct matrix (not transfer-matrix multiplication — slower in principle, far easier to verify). G_IJ at the surface sums only the top layer's 6 wave amplitudes; the full inverse encodes coupling to deeper layers.

**Depth-resolved field, layered structures.** The full inverse also contains every other layer's wave amplitudes; with `nargout>1` on a layered structure they are extracted, rather than only the top film's 6 rows that `.G`/`.GFull` read. They are stored on `GreenSolver.k3OutLayered`/`.polarizationULayered`/`.partialAmplitudeLayered` (`{nLay×1}` cells in **Structure order** — index 1 = substrate, index nLay = outermost film, reversed once from `calc.m`'s internal surface-first order) plus `.hScaleOut` (the resolved `hScale` for the evaluated velocity).

**Every layer's amplitude vector is referenced to the same global x3 coordinate as the substrate** (x3=0 at the substrate interface, x3=−H_total at the free surface): `buildBCMatrix` references each phase to whichever interface plane a boundary-condition row enforces, never to a film's own local boundaries. A single reconstruction formula is therefore valid in every layer, film or substrate (one velocity, `+k` only — like the half-space `.dispField`, which is tied to `.G`, never `.G_minus`):

```
u(x3, ci, cj) = sum_n A(n,cj) * U(ci,n) * exp(i*k3(n)*x3)
```

`ci` = displacement component, `cj` = force direction, summed over that layer's `n` partial waves (6 for a film, 3 for the substrate). A film spans `x3 ∈ [−dBelow−dThis, −dBelow]`, where `dBelow` is the summed thickness of the films strictly below it and `dThis` its own thickness (both `2π·relDepth·hScale`); `RitzSolver`'s `ti ∈ [-1,1]` convention (`ti=-1` deep, `ti=+1` shallow) maps linearly onto it: `x3(ti) = -dBelow - (ti+1)/2*dThis` (`Vis.calcGreenDispField2DLayered`). The substrate uses the same formula with its own 3-wave block, amplitudes `(1i/omegaD) * invB(6*nFilm+1:6*nFilm+3, 1:3)`, over the semi-infinite `x3 >= 0` — the half-space formula, with `nFilm=0` as the degenerate case.

This gives a machine-precision invariant: film 1 (the outermost) evaluated at its shallow edge (`ti=+1`, `x3=-H_total`) equals `GvalFull(forceDir,displDir)` exactly, since it uses the same `A_1`/`phaseVec` objects `calcGFullFromPartialWaves` uses to build `.GFull`. Displacement continuity at every film/film and film/substrate interface is enforced by the linear solve itself.

`calc.m` stores only the raw per-layer `k3`/`U`/`A` and builds no depth grid (unlike the half-space `.dispField`, a fixed `xx3` array): reconstructing each layer's extent needs `Structure.calcParams(:).relDepth`, and `GreenSolver` keeps no `Structure` copy, since the caller already has one. `Vis.calcGreenDispField2DLayered` reconstructs the field from the caller's `Structure`, and `Vis.plotGreenModalShapeLayered` renders it with `plotModalShape`'s axis stacking (see `docs/reference/architecture/visual.md`).

**relDepth → physical phase factor: the `2*pi` convention.** `relDepth` (h/λ, the ordinary thickness-to-wavelength ratio) must become an *angular* wavenumber-scaled length before entering a phase factor `exp(-i·k3·h)`: `calc.m` uses `h = 2*pi * relDepth`, matching Ritz (`GammaMatrix/rawByDepth.m` scales k∥-bearing terms by `(2*pi)^2`/`2*pi` the same way). An error here is invisible to two common checks — half-space calls (relDepth never enters) and same-material film/substrate recovery tests (the interface algebra cancels whatever the depth scale) — so any direct manipulation of layer depth/phase inside `calc.m` must preserve this factor.

## Coordinate convention

Standard literature convention throughout (x1=propagation, x2=in-plane, x3=surface normal/depth), reached via `Material.GetStandardElastTensor(angle)` (`GetStandardDirMat(dirMat, angle)`, then the tensor rotation at angle 0) — one call per layer. `I=1, J=3` (the default) gives G_13 (TGS: force along propagation, displacement normal to surface).

## ±k symmetrisation, and the (forceDir,displDir) fan-out

In a TGS experiment the standing grating decomposes into +k and −k counter-propagating waves; the surface response involves both `G_IJ(+k)` and `G_IJ(−k)`.

- **Reciprocity: `G_IJ(−k) = G_JI(+k)`, exactly — so one solve gives both sides.** Betti's reciprocal theorem needs only the major symmetry of the stiffness, `C_ijkl = C_klij` (true for any linear elastic medium, of any crystal symmetry), a traction-free surface, welded interfaces, and fields that decay at infinity (guaranteed by the limiting-absorption damping). It gives `G_IJ(x, x′) = G_JI(x′, x)`: the displacement J at x from a unit force I at x′ equals the displacement I at x′ from a unit force J at x. Translation invariance along the surface turns this into `G_IJ(r) = G_JI(−r)` for the in-plane offset r, and Fourier transforming gives
  `G_IJ(−k∥, ω) = G_JI(+k∥, ω)`, i.e. the −k tensor is the +k tensor **transposed**.
  No material symmetry is involved, the relation is bilinear (no complex conjugation) so it holds at complex ω (with damping) too, and it holds for any layer stack and either scan mode. `calc()` therefore solves only `k1=+1` and builds `GFull_minus = permute(GFull, [2 1 3])`; an independent `k1=−1` solve agrees with this transpose to ~10⁻¹³. The same relation can be checked through a `+k`-only route: rotating the propagation direction by 180° reverses x1 and x2, so `GFull(θ+180°) = D·GFull(θ)ᵀ·D`, `D = diag(−1,−1,1)`.
- **Parity sign and what `G_eff` is.** Flipping `k1→−k1` with the elastic tensor held fixed is a reflection of the whole problem through the x2–x3 plane (`x1→−x1`): a vector component along x1 flips sign, x2/x3 components do not, so `G_IJ` picks up `(−1)^n`, where `n` = how many of `(I,J)` equal 1. On its own this relates `G_IJ` of the material to `G_IJ` of its *mirror image*. `G_eff = (G_+ + (−1)^n·G_−)/2` (parity-signed mean, generalising Maznev, Akthakul & Nelson 1999) is therefore, by reciprocity,
  `G_eff,IJ = (G_IJ + (−1)^n·G_JI)/2` at **+k** — e.g. `G13_eff = (G13_+ − G31_+)/2`.
  The symmetrisation is still needed (in TGS the standing grating drives the +k and −k waves together, and off mirror directions they respond differently), but it needs no second calculation, only the off-diagonal partner of the same tensor.
  - **Diagonal pairs, I=J** (e.g. G_33, G_11) → `G_+ = G_−` exactly, so `G_eff = G_+` always.
  - **Off-diagonal pairs, I≠J** (e.g. G_13, G_23) → `G_+ = (−1)^n·G_−` (so `G_eff = G_+`) only where `G_IJ = (−1)^n·G_JI`, i.e. where `x1→−x1` is a genuine symmetry of that material/direction (e.g. a mirror plane). Elsewhere `G13(+k)` and `G13(−k)` genuinely differ — real physics of the asymmetric cut, not numerical error (e.g. the `(−1)^n` relation holds at every angle on the Cu(001) cut and at none on a low-symmetry cut). On surfaces with a two-fold axis along the normal (Cu(001), Ni(110), NiTi/MgO) `G13_+` and `G13_eff` are identical.
  - **Where they differ.** Below the lowest limiting velocity (no bulk radiation) `A` comes only from the damping η, so `|G13(+k)| = |G13(−k)|` — at a bound-SAW peak too, on any cut — and the two sides differ only in phase. Above it (pseudo-SAW range, bulk radiation) their magnitudes differ: in the background typically by tens of per cent on strongly anisotropic cuts, and locally by up to ~100 % where one side passes near a zero. Both sides share every pole (`G(−k) = G(k)ᵀ`), so a pseudo-SAW sits at the same velocity, with the same width, on both, but its strength (the pole residue) differs — computed with GuliweRR: by a factor 1.1–1.2 on Si(111) and 2–8 on Cu(112), Cu(137), and Fe3Al(316) cuts. Maznev, Akthakul & Nelson (*J. Appl. Phys.* **86**, 2818–2824 (1999), p. 2820) report that `G13(ω,q) ≈ −G13(ω,−q)` holds closely near SAW and PSAW resonances; for the magnitudes this is exact at a bound SAW and close on mildly anisotropic Si, but not true of pseudo-SAWs in general. (Write `G = H + iA` with `H`, `A` Hermitian; `A` is the radiated and damped power, and `G13(−k) − conj(G13(+k)) = 2i·conj(A13)`.)
- **One solve per point; the option `'ComputeMinus'` only decides whether the `G_−` fields are filled** (default `true`; `false` leaves `G_minus`/`G_eff`/`GFull_minus`/`GFull_eff` empty, at the same cost). `CalcCommon.greenScoreSpectrum` (behind every `'full'` `calcGreen()`) returns the complex `G13_eff` that the existence score reads and its magnitude `|G13_eff|` (`greenAbsG13Eff`/`greenAbsG13EffMap`), on the one axis and at the one η (3e-3); it is the only Green data a Calc result stores. The one-sided `G13_+` (or any other component) is recomputed on request by `greenComponent`/`greenComponentMap` (`forwardCalcs.md`). Why the existence scores read `G13_eff` rather than `G13_+`: `forwardCalcs_modeClassification.md`, "`SawSpectrum` — the shared late-time machinery".
- **The `(forceDir,displDir)` fan-out is almost free** — `B⁻¹` and the partial-wave data (`k3`, `U`) do not depend on which component was requested, only the final extraction does. `.GFull`/`.GFull_minus`/`.GFull_eff` (`[3×3×nVel]`) give all nine components, both signs of k, from one solve; `GFull(I,J,:)` matches a dedicated `(forceDir=I, displDir=J)` call's `.G` exactly. This is why a `'full'` Calc angle needs one solve only: the complex `G13_eff` for the score and the displayed `|G13_eff|` come from the same call (the Calc classes keep only that; any other component costs one further solve, on request). Each velocity point is also solved independently, so a solve on a longer axis reproduces a shorter axis's samples to rounding — what lets a sweep solve every angle on one common axis and still score each angle on its own.
- **Velocities are even in propagation direction (`v(θ)=v(θ+180°)`, exactly, for Christoffel/Ritz/bulk waves — Γ(n)=Γ(−n) for any elastic tensor) — Green's `G_+` is NOT.** `G_-(θ) = −G_+(θ+180°)` for G_13 (odd in-plane-index parity), with no sign flip for G_33 (even). So `|G13_+|` at θ+180° is `|G31_+|` at θ, not `|G13_+|` at θ: a 0–180° sweep's `|G13_+|` map does not simply repeat, but its full tensor (`GFull`, all nine components) holds the other half, and `G13_eff` at every angle comes from the same solve. Mirroring a one-sided `G13_+` map across 180° is the easy mistake — check it before short-cutting any Green-based angular sweep.

## Near-isotropic materials

**Note**: this section covers the degenerate-*eigenvector-basis* fix only. Partial-wave *selection* for degenerate or near-degenerate roots is handled separately by limiting absorption ("Algorithm outline", step 3). A small residual remains below the transverse bulk velocity of an isotropic half-space (points still moving by up to ~2×10⁻⁴ under a one-ulp nudge of `velRange`): the inherent accuracy of resolving a repeated polynomial root with `roots()`, which the closed-form eigenvector fix does not remove. Solving the sextic as a 6×6 linear eigenvalue problem (Stroh 1962, Eq. 4.2) instead of by polynomial roots could remove it; the residual is far below physical relevance.

An exactly (or nearly) isotropic material makes the shear branch degenerate at *every* velocity below the longitudinal velocity, not just along isolated symmetry directions. `eig()`'s basis choice within a degenerate eigenspace is then numerically arbitrary and discontinuous (dependent on LAPACK round-off), which would corrupt the boundary-condition matrix and most of the Green curve.

The fix is a closed form. Isotropic elasticity's degenerate shear eigenspace is `Γ(k)v = μ(k·k)v + (λ+μ)k(k·v)` (bilinear, no conjugation), so any `v` with `k·v=0` is an eigenvector for any complex k3. Because this codebase's Christoffel solve always has `k2≡0` (the angle is folded into the elastic-tensor rotation upstream), the P-SV-SH decomposition reduces to two fixed, always orthogonal, continuous-in-k vectors: `eSH=[0,1,0]`, `eSV=cross(kVec,eSH)`. `Calc_U_allVel` uses this closed form (exact for isotropic materials, an improving approximation as the anisotropy ratio → 1) instead of `eig()` whenever the shear-anisotropy ratio is below `NEAR_ISO_WARN_THRESH=1.01` *and* the k3 roots are numerically degenerate, screened per layer by the same anisotropy-ratio check that raises the `GreenSolver:calc:nearIsotropic` warning. The ratio is rotation-invariant, so for isotropic and cubic constants it is read from `ElastConsts.shearAnisotropyRatio` (stored exactly at construction: 1, or `max(A_Z, 1/A_Z)`); only lower symmetries, which have no closed form, are scanned per call (`ElastConsts.CalcAnisotropyRatio` of the layer's rotated tensor, 15° step, about a millisecond). The warning is purely informational (it names the layer that used the closed form); the result is not corrupted. `calcMode='ritzOnly'` remains a valid, cheaper choice for isotropic materials (no angular dependence, so Ritz alone suffices).

**Warning deduplication:** a sweep calls `GreenSolver.calc` once per point, so `calc.m` silences the warning after its first firing via `warning('off','GreenSolver:calc:nearIsotropic')`. Each thread-pool worker keeps independent warning and persistent state, so no per-session mechanism can do better than once per worker: the warning appears exactly once in serial use (single `calc()` calls, GUI use) and at most once per worker in a parallel sweep (e.g. ≤12 times on a 12-worker pool), however many points the sweep has. `warning('on','GreenSolver:calc:nearIsotropic')` re-enables it.

## Conditioning: near-singular boundary-condition matrices

The boundary-condition matrix `B` (step 4 above) is genuinely close to singular at some `(angle, velocity)` points, for at least two distinct reasons:

- **A real guided mode sits there** — the SAW-pole mechanism described above. Expected, and exactly what makes that point interesting.
- **A thick film at high `|k∥|`** — in `'isoFreq'` mode the effective layer depth `hScale = omega0/v` *grows* as `v` falls, so at the low-velocity/high-k∥ end of a sweep a thick film's evanescent partial waves can span >15 orders of magnitude within one matrix, independent of any real mode.

Either way, MATLAB's per-point `MATLAB:nearlySingularMatrix`/`MATLAB:singularMatrix`/`MATLAB:illConditionedMatrix` (the `RCOND = NaN` variant of a non-finite `B`) warnings are suppressed inside `calc.m` (a sweep could otherwise print thousands of near-duplicate lines) and replaced by an explicit `rcond(B)` check against `nWaves*eps` — the standard "numerically singular" tolerance, in the style `rank()` uses, scaled by the actual matrix size; a non-finite `B` counts as `RCOND = 0`. If any velocity point in a `calc()` call is flagged, **one** consolidated `GreenSolver:calc:nearSingular` warning is issued at the end of the call, summarising the affected velocity range, point count and worst RCOND.

Wherever a sweep makes one `GreenSolver.calc` call per angle (e.g. `CalcIsofreqAngSweepLayered`), this gives per-angle reporting with no extra bookkeeping. It is a diagnostic message rather than an inspectable data structure, since the affected region is normally obvious on the plotted curve or map.

**What is solved.** The half-space case forms `inv(B)` (3×3). The layered case never forms the full inverse: only columns 1–3 are read, so it solves `invB = B \ E3` with `E3 = eye(nWaves,3)` — the same algebra, one LU factorisation instead of an LU plus `nWaves` triangular solves, and the conditioning is neither reduced nor hidden.

**Fix for the thick-film case: `equilibrate()`.** Evanescent decay across many orders of magnitude is a **scaling** problem, not a genuine rank deficiency: `B`'s column norms can span a ratio of many tens of orders of magnitude for a thick film (`relDepth` of order 20), enough for `rcond(B)` to report catastrophic near-singularity even though the raw solution is correct. `rcond` is the wrong instrument for this failure mode.

In a layered solve, when the raw `rcond(B)` trips the threshold at a point, `calc.m` equilibrates `B` (`[P,R,C]=equilibrate(B); Beq=R*P*B*C;`) and recovers the first three columns of the exact inverse as `invB = C*(Beq\(R*P*E3))` (exact algebra: `(RPBC)⁻¹ = C⁻¹B⁻¹P⁻¹R⁻¹`, so `B⁻¹ = C·(RPBC)⁻¹·RP`). The equilibrated result is kept, and `rcond(Beq)` replaces `rcond(B)` in the near-singular check, only if `rcond(Beq) > rcond(B)`; otherwise the plain `B \ E3` is used. The improvement in `rcond` can be tens of orders of magnitude. It is **gated behind the cheap `rcond(B)` check** already needed for the warning, because equilibrating every point costs measurable time for the well-conditioned majority with no benefit; the well-conditioned path is untouched.

An unrecoverable case remains at *extreme* `relDepth` (of order 100 and more for a thick film): `exp(Im(k3)·h)` overflows to `Inf` while `B` is being built, so the data are lost at source and no scaling can help. `equilibrate()` errors on non-finite input, so this case falls back to the raw `rcond(B)` and `B \ E3` path, counted in the consolidated warning, rather than crashing. Such a `relDepth` is far outside the `legO>=2x relDepth` rule (`docs/pitfalls.md`) for any practical `legO`, i.e. outside the intended domain.

**A second evanescent-overflow risk, specific to depth-resolved reconstruction rather than the solve.** `equilibrate()` recovers a correct `invB`, and hence correct `k3OutLayered`/`polarizationULayered`/`partialAmplitudeLayered`, even for a badly scaled `B`. But reconstructing the field at an *interior* depth (`Vis.calcGreenDispField2DLayered`) evaluates `exp(i·k3(n)·x3)` for each partial wave separately, possibly far from the global `x3=0` reference. For a thick film, or a point deep below the substrate interface, a single term's exponential can overflow to `Inf` even though its amplitude `A(n,cj)` is correspondingly tiny and the summed field is finite (`Inf × tiny` gives `Inf`/`NaN`, not the small residual it should). `G_IJ` itself is unaffected, since it only evaluates phase at the boundary planes inside the equilibrated solve. At extreme `relDepth` (of order 100 and more), `partialAmplitudeLayered` is non-finite within the near-singular velocity range that the consolidated warning flags (where `B` has non-finite entries before `equilibrate()` can run), while the call completes and the reconstruction is clean elsewhere. Referencing each partial wave to the boundary it decays *away from* (where it is largest, so every exponential has modulus ≤ 1), rather than to the global `x3=0`/`x3=-H_total` planes, would remove the overflow.

**Where the overflow can realistically be reached.** Beyond a deliberately thick film, the realistic route is `'isoFreq'` at low velocity: the effective depth is `relDepth·omega0/v`, so a relDepth-20 film at `v = omega0/5` already behaves like relDepth 100. Thin-film stacks stay far from it (checked on a four-layer thin-film stack with films ≤ 3 wavelengths thick, both scan modes, η down to 1e-5: no point flagged), so the local referencing is not implemented. It would not be faster, and `.partialAmplitudeLayered` would have to carry a per-wave reference depth (converting back to the global x3 reintroduces the overflow); `G` itself would be unchanged to rounding. The rule, should it be needed: a film wave with `Im(k3) ≥ 0` is referenced at its film's top face, one with `Im(k3) < 0` at its bottom face, and the substrate stays at `x3 = 0`; every factor in `B` then has modulus ≤ 1.

## Scan modes: isoWavevec vs. isoFreq

`G_IJ(k∥, ω)` depends on wavevector and frequency as independent variables. For a half-space (non-dispersive) only the ratio `v=ω/|k∥|` matters and both modes give identical results — a useful built-in validation check. For a layered medium they differ, because the layer dispersion parameter `k∥·h` enters differently:

| | isoWavevec | isoFreq |
|---|---|---|
| Fixed | `\|k∥\|=1` | `ω=omega0` |
| Swept | `ω → v` | `\|k∥\|=omega0/v` |
| Matches | TGS at fixed grating vector q | fixed real frequency; gives the phase-velocity surface at that frequency |
| Layer dispersion parameter | `k∥·h` constant across the sweep | `k∥·h=(omega0/v)·h`, changes every step |

The inner algorithm (sextic k3-finder, polarisations, group velocities, partial-wave selection) is *exactly identical* between the two modes at the same `v` — normalised units mean `ω_norm=v` either way. The only difference is the phase factor's effective layer thickness: `h_norm = relDepth` (isoWavevec, constant) vs. `h_norm = relDepth * omega0/v` (isoFreq, varies with v). The mode is chosen with the Name-Value options after the five positional arguments, e.g. `GreenSolver.calc(structure, angle, velRange, 1, 3, 'ScanMode', 'isoFreq', 'Omega0', w0)`; `'Omega0'` (the reference velocity of the fixed frequency, in km/s) defaults to `max(velRange)`. Two further options are `'ComputeMinus'` (default `true`, see above) and `'Eta'` (the damping, default 3e-4).
