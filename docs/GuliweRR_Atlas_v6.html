Overview

GuliweRR

Guided Linear Waves by Ritz–Rayleigh. A MATLAB toolbox, with a one-to-one Julia version, that calculates elastic-wave propagation in anisotropic, layered media — surface-guided waves (surface acoustic waves, SAW) on a half-space or film(s)-on-substrate stack, and Lamb-wave dispersion on a free, layered plate. It comprises a module for defining and transforming the elastic (stiffness) tensor; three plane-wave solvers — Christoffel, Ritz, and Green; forward-calculation modules that combine them for each of these domains and for angular or frequency sweeps; a GUI for analysing the results and wave properties; and an experimental inverse procedure that recovers the tensor from measured velocities.

Experimental parts

Inverse module. The inverse module is validated on synthetic data only; the interface may change. Its pseudo-surface-wave tracking is new. See Inverse characterisation.

Classification and spot-averaged maps. Surface-wave classification (Mode classification) and the spot-averaged Green maps (ProbeKernel, calcSpotGreenMap) are experimental as well. All three parts work and are tested, but their results and interfaces may change.

Brief physics background: the plane-wave solution

All three solvers look for plane-wave solutions of the elastic equation of motion, ρ üi = ∂j(Cijkl ∂l uk), in the form uk = ûk(x3) ei(kx1 − ωt): harmonic along the propagation direction x1, uniform along x2, with phase velocity v = ω/k. They differ only in how they find the depth profile û(x3), which is why their results are directly comparable at the same propagation direction. The simplest case is a bulk wave in an unbounded crystal, solved by Christoffel: with no boundaries, the profile is a plane wave too, and the problem reduces to a 3×3 eigenvalue problem, det[Cijkl nj nl − ρv²δik] = 0, whose roots are the three bulk velocities (one quasi-longitudinal, two quasi-shear) along the direction n.

Guided waves need a bounded domain — a free surface, layer interfaces, or both — and here the profile is no longer a single plane wave. Ritz and Green solve this in two different ways:

  • Ritz expands the profile in Legendre polynomials in each layer of a finite slab with a rigid bottom and makes the energy stationary, which gives one matrix eigenvalue problem, Kc = ω²Mc, for all modes of the slab at once. In multi-layered systems, displacement continuity at the interfaces is imposed, while traction continuity comes out of the energy principle only approximately. The traction-free surface is likewise satisfied only approximately, and a finite slab cannot represent energy leaking into the bulk.
  • Green builds the profile exactly from Christoffel's partial waves in each layer (the substrate keeps those that decay or radiate downwards), imposes every boundary condition, and returns the surface response G(k, ω) to a harmonic surface force. Surface waves appear as its peaks, and leaky waves as broadened ones.

Given a material stack (elastic constants, density, thickness, orientation) and a propagation direction, GuliweRR's forward calculations return wave velocities, mode identities, and — for SAW — the surface Green's function. Three solvers answer complementary questions at the same angle; Mode Classification folds them together, but it is an intermediate step, not the endpoint:

Minimal working example — half-space SAW velocity

run('guliwerr/init.m');

ec  = ElastConsts([108, 26.4], 1);     % isotropic Al: [C11,C44] GPa, symmNo=1
mat = Material(ec, 2.70);               % rho g/cm^3
cp  = CalcParams(20, 40);               % relDepth=20 (depth in wavelengths), legO=40 (Legendre order)
str = Structure(mat, cp, 'al', 500);

sweep = Calc.angSweepHS(str, 0, 'ritzOnly', 'saw_only', []);
sweep = sweep.calc();
vSAW  = sweep.sawVelMap{1}(1);          % km/s

juliaPort/

Two implementations: MATLAB and Julia

GuliweRR exists in two versions that are released together, one to one. MATLAB is the user-facing reference version, and the one the rest of this Atlas describes. Julia (package GuliwerrJ, in juliaPort/) is the computational version: the same calculations and the same results, faster.

Indicative: best of repeated runs on one Apple-silicon machine that was not otherwise idle. The Julia version runs its linear algebra on Apple's Accelerate library on macOS and on OpenBLAS elsewhere, so the factors will differ on other hardware. The gain is largest for Green's function, on one thread and in parallel sweeps alike, where many small solves dominate. Ritz gains a solid factor on simple cases, but little where one large complex eigenproblem dominates, as for the low-symmetry (111) stack.

Same results

Every calculation module of the Julia version is tested against values dumped from MATLAB (golden tests). On the test fixtures, single solves agree to 1e-8 relative or better (mostly far better), whole sweeps to 1e-6, and the surface-wave classification accepts and rejects the same peaks. A change to MATLAB results is ported to Julia and re-verified before either version is published.

What differs

Some functionality, mainly around plots and GUIs, is not fully ported yet: saving and reloading results (Calc.saveResult, Gui.viewSaved) and a few viewer conveniences are MATLAB-only. Julia's plots and GUIs use Makie.

guliwerr/core/

Building blocks

Five classes that describe the problem — material, mesh resolution, layer stack — and how a sweep is parallelised, plus the experimental ProbeKernel (the weight of a finite measurement spot, used by the spot-averaged Green maps; see core.md). None does any wave physics itself.

Full detail → docs/reference/architecture/core.md

ElastConsts — stiffness tensor storage and rotation

  • Three always-synchronised representations: vector (symmetry-specific), voigt (6×6), tensor (3×3×3×3) — rebuilt together, never drift apart. The constructor accepts any of the three directly and auto-converts — callers never say which one they're passing.
  • RotateTensor: full-tensor rotation via four sequential mode-n matrix products, not an 8-nested-loop sum — cheap enough for thousands of calls in an angular sweep.
  • SymmClassLibrary lists the ten symmetry classes and how many independent constants each expects, from isotropic (2) to triclinic (21).
  • SetNewSymmClass rebuilds under a different class from the Voigt matrix, with no compatibility check. Safe only to a class that contains the current one (for example isotropic → cubic → … → triclinic), which just frees previously-tied entries; going to a class that does not contain it silently discards entries without checking that they fit the tighter symmetry. Class numbers are not ordered by the number of constants.
  • Derived coefficients: directional Young's modulus (GetYoungsModulus), isotropic Voigt/Reuss/Hill averages, and cubic/tetragonal/hexagonal ↔ physical-mode conversions (each with a deconversion inverse).

Material — two coordinate conventions, for two different jobs

A Material is one layer's physics: its elasticity (ElastConsts), its density, and its crystal orientation. Two sets of coordinates describe directions in it, one for each job:

  • Input coordinates — dirMat — for specifying an orientation. Rows are direction cosines, ordered by physical importance.
  • Computational coordinates — the internal solver frame, for computing: x1 = propagation, x2 = in-plane, x3 = surface normal/depth (z = out-of-plane), the typical convention in the literature this project cites (Every, Maznev).

Material.GetStandardDirMat is the single conversion point from input coordinates. Indexing into one set of coordinates as if it were the other silently mislabels which physical direction you're reading.

dirMat rowMeaningComputational coordinate
dirMat(1,:)surface normal (plane)x3
dirMat(2,:)propagation direction referencex1
dirMat(3,:)in-plane completion — cross(row1,row2)x2

CalcParams — Legendre-basis mesh resolution

The Ritz solver's mesh, one per layer. It also holds a film layer's real thickness (realThickness), converted to relDepth per wavelength by Structure.ApplyWavelength (see Structure below). legOrder (Legendre truncation order) should satisfy legOrder ≥ 2×relDepth — see Pitfalls. Precomputes the two integral matrices (legDiag, legNondiag) the Ritz eigenproblem needs, once per construction.

Structure

  • Structure: ordered stack of (Material, CalcParams) pairs, counted from the bottom: layer 1 = substrate (bottom), layer nLay = free surface (top), where nLay is the number of layers.
  • Layer thickness. A film is given its real thickness (realThickness on its CalcParams, e.g. in µm). The solvers only use the wavelength-relative relDepth, so ApplyWavelength converts it, relDepth = realThickness / wavelength, at every sweep point. The substrate is nominally semi-infinite: its relDepth is a truncation depth set directly (SetRelDepth), not derived from a thickness. Worked example: examples/tutorials/tutorial_06_film_on_substrate.m; details in core.md, "Structure".
  • The four data classes (ElastConsts, Material, CalcParams, Structure) are value classes in MATLAB and immutable structs in Julia: every setter returns a modified copy, so assign it — obj = obj.AddLayer(...) in MATLAB, s = AddLayer(s, ...) in Julia.

ParallelPolicy

  • ParallelPolicy: thread pool (not process pool), deliberately — shares memory rather than duplicating broadcast data (elastic tensors, eigenvector caches) per worker. "auto" switches on at ≥20 angles. Stateless, and sits outside the ElastConsts/Material/CalcParams/Structure composition entirely — used by the sweep classes, not by Structure itself.
guliwerr/solvers/christoffel/

Christoffel

Bulk-wave velocities and polarisations in a homogeneous medium along one direction — the cheapest of the three solvers, no boundary condition, no depth structure. By definition, the equation for a plane harmonic wave.

Full detail → docs/reference/architecture/christoffel.md

Γ(i,k) = C(i,j,k,l) · n(j) · n(l) / ρ

Γ is the Christoffel matrix (not related to the Ritz GammaMatrix class, which shares only the name). Its three real eigenpairs (v², g) are the quasi-longitudinal L and quasi-shear T1/T2 bulk waves. Christoffel feeds the forward pipeline two things Ritz/Green don't give directly:

  • Limiting velocities (LimitingVels) — bulk group velocities confined to the sagittal plane, bounding true SAW existence from above.
  • Polarisation vectors at those limiting velocities, used by ModeClassification to associate each limiting-velocity entry with a Ritz mode, by projecting the mode's surface displacement onto the bulk polarisation.

LimitingVels.findRoots: coarse angular scan (~300 samples) → islocalmin flags candidates (catches tangential grazes, not just sign crossings) → fminbnd refines each → accepted only if residual is small relative to the in-plane group-velocity magnitude.

guliwerr/solvers/ritz/

Ritz

The primary forward solver: SAW mode velocities and eigenvectors via the Ritz–Rayleigh variational method. Versatile by design: the same Legendre/Ritz framework covers a bare half-space, an arbitrary multi-layered half-space (film-on-substrate stack), and — via the sibling RitzSolverLamb sharing the same base — a homogeneous or multi-layered free plate (see Plate). Ritz is a fast way to obtain the whole modal spectrum at once. It is very convenient for finding true guided waves, but by design less so for waves radiating into the bulk (pseudo-surface waves): a finite slab cannot carry the leaked energy away. These can be obtained as well, at the price of a deeper domain (higher computational demands) and a more involved mode characterisation.

Full detail → docs/reference/architecture/ritz.md

Depth-dependent displacement expanded in a single high-order Legendre basis spanning the whole depth domain (one element, not many small FEM-style elements). Energy minimisation turns the PDE into a generalised eigenvalue problem:

K α = ω² M α

K = elastic constants × Legendre-derivative integrals; M = density-normalised mass; α = the reduced Legendre coefficient vector actually solved for, after boundary-condition constraints are projected out — same symbol the papers use. In the normalised convention (|k∥|=1), phase velocity comes out numerically equal to frequency.

ClassRole
RitzSolverbase — eigenproblem solve, group velocity, displacement, constraint matrix
RitzSolverSAWhalf-space/layered — rigid-bottom BC, mode characteristics
RitzSolverLambfree plate — see Plate

The 21-to-15 elastic-constant reduction

The field is harmonic in propagation (x1) and homogeneous in the in-plane transverse direction (x2) — so the field never varies along x2, and the strain component ε₂₂ = ∂u₂/∂x₂ (engineering strain, Voigt index 2) is identically zero. Every elastic constant coupling to it — any Voigt-index-2 term, CI2/C2J, 6 of the 21 independent triclinic constants — contributes nothing to the strain energy and drops out before the Ritz computation. Convention-dependent: must always drop whichever Voigt index corresponds to the current completion axis.

Mode characteristics from the eigenvector, cheaply

Besides the velocities, each solve returns every mode's Legendre coefficient vector α, from which the displacement field (depth profile and polarisation) follows at negligible cost. α also characterises the mode:

  • Tracking through sweeps. A simple scalar product of α vectors at neighbouring sweep points (modal assurance criterion, MAC) links each mode to its continuation.
  • Surface character. The out-of-plane displacement at the free surface (oopSurfDispl) tells how strongly the mode shows at the surface.
  • Group velocity and depth sensitivity. Both follow from derivatives of K by first-order perturbation, with no extra solve. Comparing group and phase velocity gives the mode's frequency dispersion (freqDisp): a half-space has no length scale, so a true surface wave is non-dispersive, while a slab mode is set by the domain depth and is not. depthInsensitivity says how little the velocity moves with the artificial domain depth — large for modes confined near the surface.

RitzSolverSAW computes these per-mode quantities but does not classify; which modes are surface waves is decided in Mode classification.

The Ritz spectrum

With the full modal set known, the surface response to an excitation force can be rebuilt as a damped sum over the modes, at no meaningful extra cost. This reproduces the time-domain behaviour to some extent and shows the effect of the excitation. The result is the Ritz spectrum S(v), which mimics the surface response that Green's function computes directly (next section). Its definition, storage, and the score built on it: forwardCalcs_modeClassification.md, "The modal sum (the Ritz spectrum)". S(v) is used for surface-wave existence (the 'ritz' score, see Mode classification), for the contributing modes of a peak (RitzScore.decompose), and as the response map in the plots and viewers (Vis.plotRitzSpectrumMap, the "Ritz spectrum" graph type) — the only response background of a 'ritzOnly' result.

guliwerr/solvers/green/

Green's function

Surface elastodynamic Green's function GIJ(k∥, ω): the displacement response (in direction J), to a spatially and temporally harmonic (plane-wave) force (in direction I), both at the free surface, at a given in-plane wavevector and frequency. abs(G) is the primary observable — peaks mark surface waves: a singularity for a true SAW, a high (finite) peak for a leaky pseudo-SAW (pSAW, a surface wave that radiates energy into the bulk), and smaller features at bulk-wave threshold slownesses, most visible when that bulk mode carries some out-of-plane displacement.

Full detail → docs/reference/architecture/green.md

Algorithm

  1. Find k3 — for fixed in-plane slowness, the Christoffel determinant is a 6th-degree polynomial in k3 ("the characteristic sextic equation," Every 1997 Eq. 10), solved here directly via matrix-polynomial determinant + roots().
  2. Find polarisations U — null vector of Γ(k) − ρω²I per k3 root.
  3. Select 3 physical partial waves out of the 6 roots — complex roots that decay into the depth, and real roots (bulk waves) whose energy flows away from the surface (outgoing, by ray direction).
  4. Build the boundary-condition matrix B (traction at the free surface) and invert it.
  5. Compute GIJ from U and B−1.

Complex-frequency regularisation

At real ω, a true SAW is a singularity of G, and step 3 is conditional: deciding the energy-flow direction of a real root requires its group velocity, and where two roots coincide the choice is ill defined. Both are solved by a small imaginary part of the frequency, ω → ω·√(1+iη), used throughout the whole solve (k3, U, B, G alike).

  • The SAW singularity is regularised into a sharp, finite peak. Its position does not depend on η; its sampled height and width do — on η and on how finely the velocity axis samples the peak. The default is η = 3×10⁻⁴; the Calc classes use a larger η (below), and pitfalls.md (item 14) says what that means when comparing heights.
  • Partial-wave selection becomes unconditional — Im(k3)>0, no threshold. With Im ω > 0 no root can be real, so no root crosses the real axis as the velocity changes, and the 3/3 split into decaying and growing roots holds at every velocity. It picks the same outgoing waves as the ray-direction test at real ω.

Layered stacks (Every 2002) use one direct boundary-condition matrix covering every interface; its inverse also yields each layer's partial-wave amplitudes, from which Vis.calcGreenDispField2DLayered reconstructs the depth-resolved field.

±k symmetrisation

Velocities are even in angle — Green is not

Γ(n)=Γ(−n) for any elastic tensor, so v(θ)=v(θ+180°) exactly for Christoffel/Ritz. Green's function does not share this, due to the one-directional imposed force. Reusing the 0–180° duplication trick for a symmetrised Green quantity can be wrong.

When the excitation drives the +k and −k waves together (such as a standing grating in TGS), the relevant response is the effective Green's function G_eff ≡ (G(+k) + (−1)nG(−k))/2, where n is how many of I, J equal 1. Elastodynamic reciprocity gives GIJ(−k) = GJI(+k) for any elastic tensor, cut, and layer stack, so one +k solve gives both sides (e.g. G13eff = (G₁₃ − G₃₁)/2). The ±k responses share every pole: for a bound SAW |G₁₃(+k)| = |G₁₃(−k)|, while for a pseudo-SAW the two residues differ (computed with GuliweRR: 1.1–1.2× on Si(111), 2–8× on Cu(112), Cu(137), and Fe₃Al(316)). The cases, the derivation, and the checks: docs/reference/architecture/green.md ("±k symmetrisation").

One solve per angle in the Calc pipeline

Each angle runs one +k solve, at η = 3×10⁻³ (SawSpectrum.ETA), which gives G₁₃eff — the quantity every existence score reads. A result stores only |G₁₃eff|, the map the viewers draw; any other component is recomputed on request. Velocity axis and step: forwardCalcs.md, "The Green velocity axis"; why this η: forwardCalcs_modeClassification.md, section 5.

Near-isotropic materials

An isotropic material makes the shear branch degenerate at every velocity below the longitudinal one — eig()'s basis choice becomes numerically arbitrary. Handled by the closed-form isotropic degenerate eigenspace (eSH=[0,1,0], eSV=cross(kVec,eSH)) whenever the generalised anisotropy ratio (fastest over slowest shear velocity, squared) is below 1.01 and the k3 roots are numerically degenerate.

Forward calculations

The orchestration layer: drives Christoffel, Ritz, and Green across single points, angle sweeps, and 2D sweeps. Nothing here does its own wave physics — this layer decides which combination of solves to run, sharing what, and how to interpret the result.

The eigenvector matrix grows as (3·Σ legO)² until nMaxModes caps its columns: ~0.35–7 MB per angle — a 2D sweep multiplies that across both axes. CalcWavevecAngSweep2Layered/CalcWavevecAngSweep2Plate default to 'none' for exactly this reason. The 0–180° angle grid

The angle-sweep classes (CalcAngSweepHS, CalcAngSweepLayered, CalcAngSweepPlate) default to 91 angles (0:2:180°), not a full circle — exact, not approximate: Christoffel/Ritz/bulk velocities satisfy v(θ)=v(θ+180°) identically, so 180–360° would be pure duplication. Green's function does not share this symmetry (G13₊ at θ+180° is −G31₊ at θ), so one-sided Green maps must not be mirrored across 180°; the ±k-symmetrised G13eff map needs no extra range, since each angle's one solve gives it.

Dispersion branches

Within a sweep over wavelength at a fixed angle, the solvers return modes sorted by velocity, so mode numbers do not stay with one physical wave: in a layered stack, the fundamental and Sezawa branches cross and swap places. DispersionBranches.track follows each Ritz mode from one wavelength step to the next by eigenvector overlap, assigned one to one at each step; trackSeededBranch returns the Rayleigh-type branch alone. Vis.plotDispersionCurves draws the tracked branches. Needs retentionLevel='full'.

guliwerr/solvers/ritz/RitzSolverLamb.m

Plate (Lamb waves)

Lamb-mode velocities, group velocities, mode types, and zero-group-velocity points for a homogeneous or multi-layered free plate — same Ritz–Rayleigh method, different (free-free) boundary condition.

Full detail → docs/reference/architecture/plate.md

No Christoffel/Green phase, for two physical reasons: no Green's-function solver for a finite free plate is implemented here, and every converged Ritz mode is a genuine physical branch — both plate surfaces being traction-free are the true physical BCs, not an artificial truncation, so there's no "which mode is real" question for ModeClassification to answer.

Mode-type classification

RitzSolverLamb.modeRecognition classifies every mode by mid-plane parity: each displacement component is split into its even and odd parts about the plate's mid-plane, and the mode goes to the class holding most of its energy — Symm (u1 even + u3 odd), Asymm (u1 odd + u3 even), TH-Symm (u2 even) or TH-Asymm (u2 odd). modeTypeConfidence is the largest energy fraction minus the second: 1 for a pure mode, low for a mixed one (SH-Lamb coupling, or parity mixing on a stack without a mirror plane).

A real asymmetric stack breaks the clean picture

Without a mirror plane, the true operators don't block-diagonalise by parity at all — every real mode is an inherent mixture. Structure.isMirrorSymmetric gives an exact, threshold-free test; when it fails, CalcWavevecAngSweep2Plate.classifyBranches falls back to modeTypeConfidence (default confidenceThresh 0.3, explicitly unvalidated) evaluated locally per (wavevector, branch).

Multi-layer stacks: validated against GEWtool

The null-space projection is generic over layer count, and nothing restricts it to a single layer. Besides the single-layer checks (an isotropic plate against the classical Rayleigh–Lamb equations, an anisotropic Si(001) wafer against published, measured ZGV resonances, below), four multi-layer stacks (two bilayers, a three-layer stack with a strongly anisotropic layer off every symmetry axis, a mirror-symmetric sandwich; kd = 0.1–15) were compared with GEWtool v2.1.1 (D. A. Kiefer, doi:10.5281/zenodo.10114243), an independent open-source (ISC) spectral-element code. Frequencies of the lowest 25 modes agree to 1e-9 relative for kd > 1 (round-off in both codes grows below 0.2 MHz), group velocities to 2e-8, ZGV points to 2e-9, and the parity classes of the sandwich exactly. This is a code-to-code check, not a comparison with a measured multi-layer plate.

Zero-group-velocity (ZGV) points

Where a branch's group velocity vanishes at finite, non-zero wavevector — a sharp, long-lived resonance with strong elastic-constant sensitivity. Two-stage: candidate scan over an already-computed sweep (no extra solves), then a Newton refinement of the plate eigenproblem written as a polynomial in k, solved together with the zero-group-velocity condition (adapted from GEWtool's ZGVNewtonBeta, Kiefer, Plestenjak, Gravenkamp & Prada 2023). It holds no mode number fixed, so a branch crossing near the point does not matter.

ZGV or TGV. Every plate solve also gives the group-velocity component perpendicular to k (velsGroupTrans, Hellmann–Feynman in angle). The search runs along k at one fixed angle; each located point is flagged isTrueZGV when the perpendicular component vanishes too. On a symmetry direction of the plate (e.g. ⟨100⟩ and ⟨110⟩ of a cubic (001) plate) it does, and the point is a true ZGV point — a minimum or a saddle point of the dispersion surface. At other angles it generally does not: the point carries energy sideways and is a TGV wave (Kiefer, Mezil & Prada 2023), returned with the flag false.

Validated against measurement. On a Si(001) wafer (d = 524.6 µm, inputs as published by Kiefer et al. 2023), GuliweRR gives the S1 ZGV points at 7.7079 MHz on [100] and 7.7551 MHz on [110]. These are their computed values to every digit given, and within 0.01 % of their measured 7.7072 and 7.7545 MHz. At 18.4° from [100] it gives their TGV wave: 7.7265 MHz with a transverse group velocity of 0.178 mm/µs (computed 0.177, measured 0.170 mm/µs; tutorial S2, Part 5).

Mode classification

Experimental

Surface-wave classification is checked against the measured maps published in Grabec et al. 2024 and the UTGS paper (arXiv:2510.10696), and there is no general agreement on which spectral peaks count as surface waves in strongly anisotropic materials. Thresholds, scores, and the interface may change.

Turns the solvers' output into a classified list of surface waves — "where is the Rayleigh wave, where are the pseudo-SAWs" — rather than a bare list of eigenvalues. Three questions are kept apart and answered in this order: existence (is there a surface wave an experiment would see?) from one late-time score per spectral peak; identity (which Ritz mode represents it?) from Ritz; the label (Rayleigh or pseudoRayleigh) last, from one velocity compared with the slowest shear limit.

The late-time idea. A measured TGS signal is read after its first nanoseconds, when the bulk waves have left the measurement spot but a surface wave is still there (UTGS paper, arXiv:2510.10696). Every score does the same to a calculated spectrum, on G₁₃eff at a measurement-like damping, and is normalised within its own direction, so a single angle classifies exactly as the same angle inside a sweep.

Standards. Each method has two: 'high' — sensitive (every mode a good TGS map shows) — and 'low' — distinct modes only. Entries passing only the looser one are graded weak and drawn as open markers. Candidates are stored threshold-free, so switching method or standard after the calculation only re-thresholds them (the viewers' classification bar does the same):

sweep = sweep.reclassify('low');            % another standard, same method
sweep = sweep.classify('refined');          % another method, its default standard

Identity and label. Each accepted peak is matched to the nearest Ritz mode with strong out-of-plane surface displacement; a weak match never removes a peak. The label compares the peak velocity with the slowest shear limit: below it Rayleigh, above it pseudoRayleigh.

Layered stacks use 'green' and 'ritz' (no 'refined'), both 'low' by default, searching the velocity band of all layers; 'low' keeps sagittal-type waves (Rayleigh, Sezawa), 'high' adds Love-type ones, and layered 'ritz' never reports leaky waves. Entries carry a single SAW type.

Validation against measured TGS maps: Ni and Fe₃Al cuts (UTGS paper) for the half-space, NiTi/MgO (Grabec et al. 2024) for layered stacks. Numbers, thresholds and design: forwardCalcs_modeClassification.md.

Results saved under older classification rules still load but cannot be reclassified — recompute them.

guliwerr/inverseCalcs/ · Inv

Inverse characterisation

Experimental

Validated on synthetic data only; script-driven, and the interface may change.

Recovers the elastic constants of one selected layer (a half-space or one layer of a stack) from measured velocities along an angular sweep: SAW, pseudo-SAW, and bulk limiting velocities, optionally with pulse-echo phase velocities and a soft-fixed bulk modulus. The approach follows Grabec et al. 2024.

  1. Association. Each measured point is paired with its calculated counterpart at the initial guess — a Ritz eigenvector for SAW and pseudo-SAW points, a Christoffel root for limiting points. The pairing works angle by angle, not branch by branch, so a mode seen only in some directions can still be used.
  2. Sensitivity. The sensitivity matrix S = 2GᵀG (G the derivatives of the calculated velocities with respect to the elastic constants, from perturbation theory) is diagonalised into uncorrelated combinations of the constants. Only those combinations the data actually determine are optimised; their eigenvalues also give the error estimate of each constant.
  3. Optimisation. A least-squares minimisation of the velocity residuals over these combinations. During it the pairing is kept by eigenvector similarity (MAC), so a mode whose character changes slightly stays bound to its measured points. The forward sweep is not re-run: the Ritz stiffness matrix is linear in the elastic constants.

Several cuts of the same crystal can be inverted jointly for one shared elasticity. The association is script-built for now; a graphical mode-association tool is planned. Start from examples/tutorials/tutorial_09_inversion_synthetic.m; the calls are in functionReference.md.

A recurring gotcha: cla() doesn't reset axis-limit modes

cla() clears an axes' children only, not its limit-mode state. If a previous draw ever called xlim/ylim/clim explicitly, MATLAB leaves the axes in 'manual' mode — a later redraw that never sets its own limits silently inherits the previous draw's stale range. The map viewers reset the relevant limit modes (XLimMode/YLimMode/CLimMode) to 'auto' right after cla().

docs/pitfalls.md

Pitfalls

A selection of common mistakes, physics first. The full list, with reasons, is in docs/pitfalls.md; its IDs are shown on the cards. Severity: 🔴 silent wrong result · 🟠 misreading · ⚪ loud or cost only.

Physics

PHY2 🔴Layered: branches coexist

Higher-order (Sezawa-type) branches coexist with the fundamental, and every point is classified on its own, so a per-point pick can jump between branches. A substrate's second shear limit can mimic a SAW. Follow one branch with DispersionBranches tracking (Dispersion branches).

PHY1 🟠Existence is a threshold on a continuum

Branches fade gradually; type and the angle where a branch ends depend on the standard. Finer velocity resolution does not sharpen an end — compare scores with the standard instead.

PHY3–5 🟠Reading Green peaks

A leaky pseudo-SAW may have no single credible Ritz mode; a tall peak can be a bulk shoulder or Van Hove edge; a Rayleigh pole's height and width depend on η, its location does not.

PHY6 🟠Layered: Love and leaky waves

The layered default 'low' keeps sagittal waves only — Love waves need 'high'. 'ritz' never reports leaky layered waves; use 'green'.

Method-related numerics

MTH1 🔴legO ≥ 2×relDepth

Under-resolution fails silently: a ratio of 1 gives velocity errors of up to a few per cent, depending on material and cut, that look like real physics. 'ritz' needs 2.3×. Plates are the exception: there a large legOrder loses digits.

MTH2–4 🔴Your own labelling near minShear

Never label Rayleigh vs pseudoRayleigh from a Ritz velocity; decide branch membership from one quantity; extend a findpeaks window past any boundary, or a peak at the edge is invisible.

MTH5–7 🟠Reading mode classification (experimental)

Existence is decided by the active method's score against its standard — not by oopSurfDispl, matchScore, or relPeakHeight. Each method has its own scale: compare scores only within one method, and prefer 'high'/'low' to numbers. Values and reasons: forwardCalcs_modeClassification.md.

MTH8 🟠Stored Green data are at η = 3×10⁻³

Not at GreenSolver's default 3×10⁻⁴, on a 6 m/s axis: plotted peaks are sampled. A height threshold tuned on a default-η spectrum does not apply.

MTH13 ⚪Stale results

A result classified under an older rule cannot be reclassified — recompute it.

Standard numerics

NUM1 🔴Complex transpose

' conjugates, .' doesn't. Always .' for G, eigenvectors, k3 unless conjugation is actually intended.

NUM2 🔴ElastConsts: pass symmNo

From a Voigt matrix or tensor the symmetry class is guessed from distinct values, so equal constants (cubic with c12 = c44) silently give the wrong class.

NUM3 🔴pcolor corner-not-centre

Route new heatmaps over discrete step data through Vis.pcolorCenteredGrid.

NUM4 ⚪Retention is the RAM cost

Use 'none' for any sweep step that isn't the final visualisation-bound one.

NUM5–6 ⚪Parallel runs

useParallel uses a thread pool, sharing memory rather than duplicating broadcast data; "never" for debugging. In your own parfor, pass the Ritz precompute in.

Other specifics

OTH1 🔴Input vs computational coordinates

Input coordinates (dirMat's row order) ≠ computational coordinates (the internal x1/x2/x3 solver frame). Check which one an array is in before indexing directly.

OTH2 ⚪Never addpath(genpath(...))

genpath recurses into @ClassName/ and breaks MATLAB's class lookup. Always run('guliwerr/init.m').

OTH3 ⚪Saved results are working files

A saved result is tied to the class definitions it was saved with; load it with the release named in meta.guliwerrVersion, or recompute.

Physics and method

Known limitations

Limits of the physics and the method that the current version does not remove, each with its reason.

Solvers

  1. 01
    Isotropic/near-degenerate Green residual: ~2×10⁻⁴, not exactly zero.

    The inherent accuracy of resolving a repeated polynomial root with roots(); eliminating it needs a 6×6 linear eigenvalue formulation in place of polynomial roots. See docs/reference/architecture/green.md, "Near-isotropic materials".

Plate

  1. 02
    Multi-layer Plate: no measured reference.

    Checked against GEWtool (frequencies, group velocities, ZGV points, parity of a mirror-symmetric stack), but not against a measured multi-layer plate; mode-type labels of an asymmetric stack are untested.

  2. 03
    Plate branch-classification confidence threshold (0.3) is unvalidated.

    A reasonable starting point, not tuned against real multi-layer data or literature.

  3. 04
    Plate ZGV search runs along k at the angles of the sweep only.

    locateZGVs finds zero group velocity along k at one fixed angle and flags whether the component perpendicular to k vanishes too (a true ZGV point) or not (a TGV wave). A true ZGV point off the symmetry directions of a low-symmetry plate, a stationary point of ω(kx, ky) at an angle the sweep does not contain, would need a 2-D search in (k, angle), not implemented. Candidates also come from the sampled group velocity: two ZGV points closer than about one wavevector step at an avoided crossing can be missed on a coarse grid.

Mode classification

  1. 05
    No Love type, and Love waves rank low on purpose.

    Shear-horizontal motion couples only weakly into G₁₃, the component every existence score reads. On a half-space no Love type exists; on a layered stack both methods rank Love-type waves below sagittal ones (a polarisation term), so 'low' excludes them and 'high' finds most of them at the cost of false positives. A criterion from another component (for example G₁₂) is not validated — no new Green solving needed, only new analysis logic.

  2. 06
    Substrate limiting-velocity cusps can mimic a genuine SAW peak.

    The existence score's late-time gate suppresses such short-lived bulk features, but nothing explicitly excludes candidates that coincide with a substrate's own limiting velocity, and branch tracking follows Ritz modes whatever their classification. A tracked branch that stays flat and close to a substrate limiting velocity over a wide relDepth range is not confirmed as a physical mode.

  3. 07
    Layered classification is calibrated on one measured stack.

    Every layered threshold and the polarisation weight come from NiTi austenite on MgO (Grabec et al. 2024). A strongly leaky sagittal wave between the substrate's shear and longitudinal limits can pass the 'green' 'low' standard — untested against measurement; the layered 'ritz' never reports leaky waves.

  4. 08
    The 'ritz' method is weaker on extreme anisotropy and tied to its basis.

    On an extremely anisotropic synthetic cubic cut (Zener ratio 2C₄₄/(C₁₁ − C₁₂) ≈ 20), its Rayleigh wave hugging minShear keeps a stable label, but 'low' keeps only about half of that branch (strong dephasing); how well it agrees with 'green' on such a material is not yet measured. Its gates are calibrated on the baseline Ritz basis (relDepth ≈ 30, legOrder ≥ 2.3 relDepth), and the bottom-trapped gate rests on one cut.

Dispersion tracking

  1. 09
    DispersionBranches.track (Dispersion branches) can only be as good as the step-to-step overlaps.

    Branches are assigned one to one at each step. A step too coarse for a near-degenerate pair can still pair modes wrongly; finer steps are the remedy.

Inverse module

  1. 10
    The inverse module is experimental and script-driven only.

    Validated on synthetic data only; the graphical mode-association tool that ModeAssociation's point-pinning is shaped for is not yet built.
